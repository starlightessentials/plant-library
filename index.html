Okra

Abelmoschus esculentus

A warm-season pod vegetable in the mallow family — kin to the Mallow in the Medicinal Herbs guide, and to hibiscus and cotton, which its beautiful pale-yellow flowers make easy to believe. The young pods are picked tender; left alone they grow tough and woody.

Origin & Lineage

  • Okra's exact birthplace is still argued over. The Ethiopian highlands — reaching into Eritrea and Sudan — are the most favoured origin, with West Africa and South Asia also claimed; what is certain is that it was cultivated in Egypt by the 12th–13th centuries, where it was called bamya, a name still used across the Middle East.
  • It reached the Americas in the 17th century through the transatlantic slave trade — recorded in Brazil by 1658 and in the American South by the late 1680s — carried by African people who kept their foodways alive through the crossing. It remains a cornerstone of African diaspora cooking.
  • Even its English names carry the journey: “okra” comes from the Igbo ọ́kụ̀rụ̀, and “gumbo” from the Bantu ki ngombo.
  • Modern varieties branch from that heritage — heirloom Southern standards, red-podded ornamentals, and dwarf hybrids bred for pots.

Health Benefits

  • Low in calories (33 kcal per 100 g raw) and a good source of fibre — about 3.2 g per 100 g — including the mucilage, a soluble, gel-forming fibre that gives okra its famous silkiness and slows the absorption of sugar and cholesterol in the gut.
  • A good source of vitamin C (23 mg per 100 g, about 26% of the daily value), vitamin K (about 26%), folate (about 15%), magnesium and manganese.
  • Pooled analyses of small clinical trials have found modest reductions in fasting blood sugar and LDL cholesterol with okra preparations — but the trials used concentrated powders and extracts (around 3,000 mg a day), not plate-sized portions of the vegetable. Promising, and a good reason to enjoy okra often — but it is not a treatment for diabetes or high cholesterol.
  • Caution: okra may interfere with how the diabetes medicine metformin is absorbed — if you take it, keep okra at ordinary food portions, space it away from the dose, and mention it to your clinician.
  • Caution: okra contains oxalates, natural compounds found in many plants that can add to kidney stones in people prone to them, so people prone to kidney stones may want to moderate portions.

In the Garden

  • A true heat-lover: sow or transplant only once the soil is warm, and it will shrug off the hottest weeks that stall other crops.
  • The hibiscus-like flowers are visited by bees and other pollinators before each one becomes a pod.
  • A deep taproot helps it tolerate dry spells once established, and lets it draw minerals up from below the reach of shallow roots.
  • Pick every day or two at 3–4 inches — frequent picking keeps the plant producing all season.

Peppers

Capsicum (five domesticated species)

One plant family that runs the whole scale — from the sweet bell, with no heat at all, to the habanero, where a sliver flavours a pot. Peppers are a single genus, Capsicum, with five domesticated species; the everyday kinds on this page are mostly Capsicum annuum, with the habanero from Capsicum chinense. The heat comes from capsaicin, concentrated in the pale membrane inside the fruit — not, as most people guess, mainly in the seeds.

Origin & Lineage

  • Wild peppers began in the eastern Andes, in what is now Bolivia and southern Peru, and people have eaten them since at least 7,500 BC. Capsicum annuum — the species behind bells, jalapeños, poblanos and cayennes — was domesticated in Mexico some 6,000 years ago.
  • Columbus met peppers in 1492 and misnamed them after black pepper. The mistake stuck; the plant did not wait around. Within fifty years Spanish and Portuguese traders had carried it to Africa, India and Southeast Asia; it reached China by the mid-1500s and Hungary by Ottoman trade routes, where it became paprika.
  • By 1600 peppers were established from Korea to West Africa — arguably the fastest global spread of any New World food, because they slotted into the place black pepper and local hot spices already held.
  • A naming footnote: Capsicum chinense, the habanero's species, is South American through and through — a botanist wrongly assumed it came from China, and the error is preserved in its Latin name.

Health Benefits

  • Ripe red bell peppers are among the richest common sources of vitamin C — about 128 mg per 100 g (142% of the daily value), more than oranges by weight. Green bells give about 80 mg; peppers gain vitamin C and vitamin A as they ripen from green to red.
  • Red peppers are also a good source of vitamin A (about 157 µg RAE per 100 g), supporting normal vision and immunity.
  • Capsaicin, the heat compound, has one firmly established medical use: applied to the skin in creams and patches, it eases nerve and joint pain. That is a medicine use of the concentrated compound — eating peppers is not the same thing.
  • Claims that eating chillies meaningfully boosts metabolism or burns fat come from small, short-lived effects in trials; they are too slight to state as a benefit, so they are not claimed here.
  • Caution: hot peppers can aggravate reflux and sensitive stomachs. When preparing hot varieties, wear gloves if your skin is sensitive, wash hands well, and never touch your eyes — the heat sits in the pale membrane, so removing membrane and seeds tames a pepper more than rinsing does.

In the Garden

  • Heat-loving and slow to start: in cooler climates, sow indoors 8–10 weeks before the last frost and transplant into the warmest, sunniest spot.
  • Peppers are self-pollinating, but bees working the flowers improve fruit set; a flowering plant draws pollinators into the garden.
  • Fruit left to ripen fully — green to red — gains sweetness and vitamins; harvesting green encourages the plant to keep producing.
  • Save seed from open-pollinated varieties, but grow hot and sweet types apart if you want the seed to come true — peppers cross-pollinate readily.

Sweet Potato

Ipomoea batatas

A sweet, starchy storage root — a root that swells to store the plant's food, and the part we eat — from tropical America, and a member of the morning glory family — the trailing vines and trumpet flowers give it away. It is not a true yam, and not a potato either: it is its own plant, and one of the most nourishing common foods in the world.

Origin & Lineage

  • Sweet potatoes were domesticated in tropical America at least 5,000 years ago, most likely in Central America; dried remains found in Peru's Chilca Canyon date back some 10,000 years.
  • Long before European ships, the plant crossed the Pacific: sweet potatoes were growing in Polynesia by about 1000–1100 CE, and genetic studies of old herbarium specimens support a pre-Columbian journey from the Peru–Ecuador coast. Even the names echo each other — Quechua kumar and Polynesian kumara / kūmara.
  • After 1492 it travelled the other way too, with Spanish and Portuguese traders carrying it to Africa, India and Asia. In Mexico and the Philippines it is still camote, from the Nahuatl camotli.
  • Nearly all modern varieties branch from that American ancestry — bred in Louisiana, Japan, Okinawa and beyond for colour, sweetness and texture.

Health Benefits

  • Exceptionally rich in vitamin A: one medium baked sweet potato provides about 1,096 µg RAE — RAE is the measure of vitamin A the body can use — roughly 122% of the daily value — in the form of beta-carotene, which the body converts as needed. Vitamin A supports normal vision and the immune system.
  • A good source of fibre — about 3.8 g in a medium potato — which supports digestion and steadier blood sugar. Boiled sweet potato sits in the moderate range of the glycaemic index — the scale of how quickly a food raises blood sugar — at around 41–50.
  • Provides vitamin C (about 20–25% of the daily value) and potassium (about 542 mg per medium potato), which helps keep blood pressure in the normal range as part of a balanced diet.
  • In a randomised trial, orange sweet potato improved vitamin A stores in schoolchildren — one reason it is used in public-health programmes where vitamin A deficiency is common.
  • Purple-fleshed varieties carry anthocyanins, the same family of pigments that colour blueberries; the orange varieties carry the beta-carotene. Colour is a fair guide to what a variety offers.
  • The leaves are a food in their own right. Cooked as a green across Asia, Africa and Latin America, they are rich in beta-carotene, B vitamins, iron, calcium and protein, and carry more antioxidative polyphenols than many commercial vegetables.
  • Caution: sweet potatoes contain moderate oxalates, natural compounds found in many plants that can add to kidney stones in people prone to them, so people prone to kidney stones may want to moderate portions. Eating a great deal can tint the skin slightly orange — harmless, and it fades.

  • The young leaves are edible too, cooked as a green in many of the cultures that grow the roots.

Burdock

Arctium lappa

A biennial pioneer of disturbed ground — waste places, roadsides and field edges. The root is a food as much as a medicine, eaten as a vegetable in Japan, and the purple, thistle-like flowers feed bees through the summer.

Health Benefits

  • The root is a food as much as a medicine — in Japan it is eaten as a vegetable, called gobo, and it is rich in the fibre inulin.
  • European regulators recognise the root as a traditional herbal medicine to increase the flow of urine and flush the urinary tract, as an aid in minor urinary complaints.
  • The same monograph lists it for temporary loss of appetite.
  • It is also traditionally used for seborrhoeic skin conditions — oily, flaky skin.
  • Older herbals call burdock a “blood purifier” and a detox herb; that idea comes from tradition, not testing — there is no evidence it treats cancer, diabetes or infections.
  • Caution: buy burdock root rather than gathering it wild — the root is easily confused with the root of deadly nightshade (belladonna), and commercial burdock teas have been poisoned by that mix-up. Burdock is in the daisy family, so avoid it if you are allergic to daisy-family plants such as chrysanthemums, and avoid it in pregnancy, as it may stimulate the womb. Take care alongside diabetes medicines — burdock lowered blood sugar in animal studies — and herbal references advise care with blood-thinning medicines too.

Soil & Environment

  • A biennial pioneer of disturbed ground — waste places, roadsides and field edges — covering bare soil where little else is established.
  • Its deep taproot, growing to about three feet, drives down through compacted soil and helps break it up.
  • The purple, thistle-like flowers feed bees and other pollinators through the summer.
  • An honest downside: the hooked burrs — the fastening that inspired Velcro — tangle in fur and feathers and have trapped small birds and bats, and the long-lived seed makes burdock hard to remove once it settles in.

Calendula

Calendula officinalis

The classic salve flower — calendula-infused oil is the heart of many herbal salves and balms. The petals are edible too, with a mild peppery tang.

Health Benefits

  • The classic salve flower — calendula-infused oil is the heart of many herbal salves and balms, including our own Soothing Salve.
  • European regulators recognise the flower as a traditional herbal medicine for minor inflammations of the skin, such as sunburn.
  • The same monograph lists it as an aid in the healing of minor wounds — a use documented since at least the 12th century.
  • It is also traditionally used for minor inflammation in the mouth or throat, as a gargle or rinse.
  • The petals are edible, with a mild peppery tang, and have long been used to add golden colour to foods — the “poor man's saffron”.
  • Caution: calendula is in the daisy family — do not use it if you are allergic to daisy-family plants such as ragweed or chamomile. The EMA does not recommend it in pregnancy or while breastfeeding, as its safety there has not been established.

Soil & Environment

  • One of the longest-flowering plants in the garden — in bloom from late spring to the first frosts, feeding bees and other pollinators for months.
  • The flowers draw in hoverflies and lacewings, whose larvae hunt aphids — a standing patrol for the vegetable garden.
  • Grown as a companion plant among vegetables, it also works as a decoy, drawing aphids onto itself and away from crops.
  • It self-seeds freely and returns year after year in the same spot, though the seedlings are shallow-rooted and easy to pull where they are not wanted; its taproot helps loosen the soil.

Chickweed

Stellaria media

A low, mat-forming spring plant. The whole plant is edible and has long been eaten as a spring green, and it has a traditional place on the skin as well as in teas.

Health Benefits

  • Traditionally used on the skin — as a fresh poultice, wash or salve — to soothe itching, minor rashes, irritation and insect bites. This use rests on tradition; it has not been tested in controlled human trials.
  • Traditionally taken as a tea to soothe the throat and digestive tract.
  • The whole plant is edible and has long been eaten as a spring green. It contains vitamin C, flavonoids and minerals including calcium, iron and potassium.
  • Contains natural saponins, the soap-like compounds behind its soothing, slippery feel on the skin.
  • Caution: eaten in large amounts, the saponins can upset the stomach. Gather only from clean ground — plants from heavily fertilised soil can build up nitrates — and avoid medicinal use in pregnancy, as its safety has not been established.

Soil & Environment

  • Forms a dense, low mat that works as a living mulch, shading the soil, holding moisture and protecting the surface from erosion.
  • Its presence is a sign of fertile, moist soil.
  • The tiny seeds are eaten by songbirds such as sparrows, finches and doves, and the flowers give early nectar and pollen to small bees and hoverflies.
  • When it dies back or is turned into the soil, it returns the minerals it gathered to the topsoil.

Cleavers

Galium aparine

A scrambling, tangled spring herb — also called goosegrass, because geese and poultry graze the leaves. It is gathered fresh in spring, as a classic spring tonic herb in European tradition.

Health Benefits

  • A classic spring tonic herb in European herbal tradition, gathered fresh in spring.
  • Traditionally used as a gentle diuretic — to support urine flow and ease minor fluid retention and urinary discomfort.
  • Herbalists traditionally class cleavers as a lymphatic herb, used for swollen glands. This rests on traditional practice alone; there are no human trials showing that it changes lymph flow.
  • Traditionally used as a wash or poultice to calm irritated skin.
  • The young shoots have traditionally been eaten as a spring green, and the plant contains vitamin C.
  • Caution: cleavers has not had formal safety testing. It is not recommended in pregnancy or while breastfeeding, and care should be taken alongside diuretic medicines.

Soil & Environment

  • A food plant for the caterpillars of many moths and butterflies, including the hummingbird hawk-moth.
  • Birds eat the seeds, and geese and poultry graze the leaves — the source of its other name, goosegrass.
  • Its scrambling, tangled growth gives low cover and shelter for small insects and other wildlife.
  • Quickly covers bare, disturbed ground, helping to hold the soil in place.

Clover

Trifolium repens

White clover is a creeping perennial in the pea family, native to Europe and Central Asia and now naturalised in lawns and pastures worldwide, rooting as it spreads to form a soft green carpet. Its flowers and leaves are the parts traditionally used — and it should not be confused with red clover, a different plant with different uses.

Health Benefits

  • In folk herbalism, a tea of the flowers and leaves has traditionally been used as a gentle expectorant — a remedy that helps loosen phlegm — for coughs, colds and chesty congestion. This is a traditional use; white clover has barely been studied in clinical research.
  • The same flower tea was a traditional spring “tonic,” taken as a general pick-me-up in folk practice.
  • The flowers and leaves are edible and mildly nutritious, containing small amounts of vitamins A and C and a range of antioxidant plant compounds.

Soil & Environment

  • White clover’s great gift to the soil: bacteria living in nodules on its roots take nitrogen from the air and turn it into a form plants can use, steadily fertilising the clover itself and the plants growing with it — the reason clover has been sown into pastures for centuries.
  • Its creeping roots and dense cover loosen and protect the surface soil, and ground under clover is typically lively with earthworms.
  • The flowers are one of the most important nectar sources for honeybees and bumblebees in many regions.

Comfrey

Symphytum officinale

Comfrey is for the skin only — never eat it, and never drink it as a tea. Its old folk name is “knitbone”, from its traditional use on injuries, and its root and leaves contain allantoin, a compound also used in skin creams.

Health Benefits

  • Comfrey is for the skin only — never eat it, and never drink it as a tea.
  • European regulators recognise the root as a traditional herbal medicine for the skin: for the relief of minor sprains and bruises, applied as a thin layer twice a day.
  • In clinical trials of regulated pharmacy ointments made from comfrey root, it eased ankle sprains about as well as a standard anti-inflammatory gel, and helped back and knee pain — that evidence belongs to those tested preparations, not to home-made poultices.
  • Its old folk name is “knitbone”, from its traditional use on injuries — but a broken bone needs medical care, not a poultice.
  • The root and leaves contain allantoin, a compound also used in skin creams, which is part of why comfrey has long been a salve herb.
  • Caution: comfrey contains pyrrolizidine alkaloids that can seriously damage the liver. In 2001 the US Food and Drug Administration advised makers to take comfrey products meant to be swallowed off the market, and regulators require “External Use Only” warnings. Use it only on unbroken skin — never on cuts or broken or irritated skin — for no more than 10 days at a time, and not in pregnancy or while breastfeeding, or for anyone under 18. Be certain of the plant before using it: comfrey leaves have been mistaken for foxglove, which is poisonous.

Soil & Environment

  • A famous “dynamic accumulator”: its deep taproot — often several feet down — draws up potassium, calcium and phosphorus from deep in the soil where other plants cannot reach.
  • The cut leaves are rich in potassium and are used as a mulch, a compost activator and a liquid plant feed.
  • The hanging bell flowers are worked through spring and summer by long-tongued bumblebees, which can reach the nectar.
  • An honest warning for the garden: comfrey is extremely vigorous and very hard to remove once established — the sterile variety ‘Bocking 14’ is the one to plant, as it does not spread by seed.

Curly Dock

Rumex crispus

Also called Yellow Dock. A pioneer of disturbed ground with a strong taproot that can reach about four feet down, and tall stems of reddish-brown seed.

Health Benefits

  • The root is a traditional gentle laxative, recognised by the British Herbal Pharmacopoeia for constipation — its anthraquinone compounds, a family of plant compounds that stimulate the bowel, are what move the bowels.
  • The same pharmacopoeia lists the root for liver congestion and for skin eruptions associated with constipation — the old idea that clearing the bowels clears the skin.
  • Native American tribes used the root as a general tonic and “blood purifier”, and as a source of iron for women. The root does contain iron, but in moderate amounts, and dock is not a treatment for anaemia.
  • Traditionally used as an alterative and mildly tonic herb — the root was once sold as a tonic and laxative.
  • Caution: the plant contains oxalates, natural compounds found in many plants that can add to kidney stones in people prone to them. As a laxative it is for short-term use only — overuse causes diarrhoea and digestive disturbance — and it should be avoided in pregnancy and while breastfeeding, and not combined with other laxatives.

Soil & Environment

  • Its strong taproot can reach about four feet down, drawing up moisture from deep in the soil and helping to break up and aerate compacted ground.
  • The tall stems carry masses of reddish-brown seed that songbirds — sparrows and red-winged blackbirds among them — feed on.
  • The leaves are food for caterpillars, and the plant is a larval host for copper butterflies.
  • A pioneer of disturbed ground whose seeds stay viable in the soil for decades — up to sixty years — which is why dock keeps reappearing in gardens.

Dandelion

Taraxacum officinale

The dandelion is a daisy-family perennial from Europe and Asia, carried by wind and people to lawns and roadsides on every continent. Almost uniquely among familiar plants, the whole thing is used — leaves, root and flowers are all food, and the root and leaf are both traditional medicines, in different ways.

Health Benefits

  • The leaves are a nutritious green, rich in beta-carotene (which the body converts to vitamin A), vitamin K and potassium, and the whole plant contains antioxidant polyphenols — protective plant compounds.
  • The root stores inulin, a gentle fibre that passes undigested to the large intestine, where it feeds beneficial gut bacteria — the kind of fibre called a prebiotic. Roots dug in autumn can be up to 40% inulin.
  • The root’s bitter compounds are traditionally used to wake up digestion: bitter tastes prompt the flow of saliva, stomach juices and bile, and dandelion root is a recognised traditional remedy in Europe for mild indigestion, fullness and temporary loss of appetite.
  • The leaf is the part traditionally used to increase urine flow and flush the urinary tract in minor urinary complaints.
  • In laboratory and animal studies, dandelion extracts show anti-inflammatory activity; this has not been established in people.
  • In laboratory studies, dandelion extracts have prompted cell death in certain cancer cell lines — this has not been shown in people, and dandelion is not a cancer treatment.

Soil & Environment

  • The long taproot grows down through heavy, compacted soil, opening channels that improve aeration and drainage, and draws minerals up from deep layers; when leaves and roots die back, those minerals are returned to the surface soil for other plants.
  • The flowers open early in spring, when little else is blooming, and give bees, butterflies and other insects a crucial early source of nectar and pollen.
  • It is a pioneer plant — one of the first to colonise bare, poor ground — holding the soil and beginning the process that lets other plants follow.

Echinacea

Echinacea purpurea

One of North America's classic medicinal plants — better known in the garden as purple coneflower. It is a native of the rocky prairies and open woodlands of central and eastern North America.

Health Benefits

  • One of North America's classic medicinal plants — better known in the garden as purple coneflower. Native American peoples used it for coughs, colds, sore throats and toothache, and it is still the herb most people reach for at the first sign of a cold.
  • The common cold is its most studied use. Reviews of the trials find that taking echinacea may slightly reduce the chance of catching a cold — but the results are mixed, and whether it shortens a cold once you have one is still unclear.
  • European regulators accept the pressed juice of the fresh flowering herb for the short-term prevention and treatment of colds, taken for no more than ten days. Traditional uses they recognise include the root for cold symptoms and for spots and pimples from mild acne, and the juice in ointments for small superficial wounds.
  • It is sold as an immune-support herb, on the idea that it stimulates the immune system. Laboratory studies support immune activity, but the effect seen in people so far is modest.
  • Caution: echinacea is in the daisy family and closely related to ragweed. Allergic reactions are possible and can occasionally be severe, so do not use it if you are allergic to daisy-family plants such as ragweed, chamomile or chrysanthemums.
  • Caution: because it may stimulate the immune system, it can work against medicines that suppress immunity, such as those taken after an organ transplant — talk to a clinician first if you take immunosuppressants or have an autoimmune condition. Its safety in pregnancy and while breastfeeding is not established, so it is best avoided then too.

Soil & Environment

  • A native of the rocky prairies and open woodlands of central and eastern North America, growing wild across the Great Plains and eastward — a natural fit for prairie-style and wildlife plantings.
  • One of the finest pollinator plants in this guide: the US conservation service rates its value to pollinators as high, and the flowers feed bees, butterflies and skippers from midsummer into autumn.
  • Leave the seed heads standing when the flowers fade — goldfinches and other songbirds feed on the seed through autumn.
  • Happy in lean, dry, well-drained soil in full sun, and drought tolerant once established. Wild echinacea has thinned with habitat loss and over-harvesting, so garden-grown plants help take the pressure off wild stands.

Goldenrod

Solidago spp.

One of the most important late-season flowers in North America. Goldenrod is usually blamed for late-summer hay fever, but its pollen is heavy and sticky and carried by insects — wind-pollinated ragweed, flowering at the same time, is the usual culprit.

Health Benefits

  • European regulators recognise goldenrod herb as a traditional herbal medicine to increase the flow of urine and flush the urinary tract, as an aid in minor urinary complaints — a use that rests on long-standing tradition; the diuretic effect has not been proven in proper trials.
  • The monograph covers European goldenrod (Solidago virgaurea); the North American goldenrods share the same folk tradition as urinary teas.
  • When it is used this way, the monograph advises drinking plenty of fluids, in courses of two to four weeks.
  • Goldenrod is usually blamed for late-summer hay fever, but that blame is misplaced: its pollen is heavy and sticky and is carried by insects, not wind. Wind-pollinated ragweed, which flowers at the same time, is the usual culprit.
  • Caution: goldenrod is in the daisy family — do not use it if you are allergic to daisy-family plants. The EMA does not recommend it for children under 12, does not recommend combining it with synthetic water tablets (diuretics), and advises against it where fluid intake must be restricted, as in severe heart or kidney disease. It is not recommended in pregnancy or while breastfeeding, where there is no safety information.

Soil & Environment

  • One of the most important late-season flowers in North America — conservation groups list goldenrods among the top autumn nectar plants, fuelling migrating monarch butterflies and filling honey bees' winter stores.
  • Specialist bees that collect pollen from almost nothing else — several mining bees and their relatives — depend on goldenrod in the autumn.
  • More than a hundred kinds of insects have been recorded visiting the flowers, and the seed feeds goldfinches, sparrows and juncos well into winter.
  • Left standing, the dry stems shelter overwintering insects through the cold months.

Ground Ivy

Glechoma hederacea

An evergreen perennial ground cover for shady places. Before hops took over, ground ivy was used to flavour, clarify and preserve ale — one of the oldest brewing herbs in Britain.

Health Benefits

  • A traditional herb for coughs and chesty complaints — long used as an expectorant and anticatarrhal, to help loosen phlegm in coughs and bronchial complaints. Health Canada's herbal monograph allows these uses on the basis of tradition; ground ivy taken by mouth has not been tested in clinical trials.
  • Traditionally used as a stomachic — a bitter digestive aid — and as an astringent for diarrhoea.
  • Traditionally used as a diuretic, and for haemorrhoids.
  • On the skin, it has traditionally been used to help heal minor cuts and wounds and to soothe inflammation — Health Canada has a separate monograph for this use on the skin.
  • Before hops took over, ground ivy was used to flavour, clarify and preserve ale — one of the oldest brewing herbs in Britain.
  • Caution: not for use in pregnancy or while breastfeeding, and avoid it if you have a kidney condition. Ground ivy is poisonous to horses if they eat a lot of it, fresh or in hay — other livestock mostly leave it alone for its bitter taste.

Soil & Environment

  • An evergreen perennial ground cover for shady places — it mats over banks and under trees, holding the soil where grass struggles.
  • Its violet flowers open early, from March to May, and are worked by bees — a good early bee plant, visited by species such as Anthophora and Osmia.
  • The foliage is rich in iron, and the plant has been recommended as an addition to the compost heap.
  • It spreads vigorously and can swamp smaller plants, so it needs room or watching; it is listed as a noxious weed in Connecticut.

Lamb's Quarters

Chenopodium album

Valued first as a food: a wild spinach whose leaves are eaten as a green, and whose seeds — from a wild relative of quinoa — were gathered and cooked like a grain for thousands of years.

Health Benefits

  • Valued first as a food: a wild spinach whose leaves are rich in vitamins A, C and K, and a good source of calcium, potassium, iron and protein.
  • Traditionally used as a mild laxative, and as a poultice to soothe minor burns and insect bites.
  • The seeds are edible too — a wild relative of quinoa, gathered and cooked like a grain for thousands of years.
  • Caution: the leaves are high in oxalates, natural compounds found in many plants that can add to kidney stones in people prone to them, so eat in moderation — and people prone to kidney stones should limit them. Cooking, especially boiling, lowers the oxalate content.
  • Caution: it can build up nitrates from rich or contaminated soil, so gather only from clean ground, away from sprayed or heavily fertilised areas.

Soil & Environment

  • A pioneer plant — one of the first to arrive on bare, disturbed ground, where its growth helps stabilise the soil.
  • Its many seeds are an important food for birds such as finches, sparrows and doves, and for small mammals.
  • Deep roots draw up minerals from below the surface, which return to the topsoil as the plant breaks down.
  • Its foliage supports insect life, and it often appears in nitrogen-rich soil.

Mallow

Malva sylvestris

A soft-leaved flowering plant in the same family as hibiscus and okra, native to Europe, North Africa and western Asia and now found almost everywhere. Leaves, flowers and roots are all used, and the whole plant is edible; its gift is mucilage, the slippery gel it makes when soaked, which coats and soothes.

Health Benefits

  • The mucilage in the leaves and flowers coats the lining of the mouth and throat, easing irritation and the dry cough that comes with it. European regulators accept this as a traditional use, based on long-standing practice rather than clinical trials.
  • A tea or rinse held in the mouth can soothe mouth sores and a sore, irritated throat in the same way, by coating the tissue.
  • The same coating action is traditionally used to calm mild discomfort in the stomach and gut.
  • The whole plant is edible — young leaves in salads, older leaves and shoots cooked as greens — and the leaves supply vitamin C, carotenoid antioxidants (the plant pigments the body can turn into vitamin A), vitamin E compounds, and minerals such as potassium, calcium and iron. The leaves also contain small amounts of alpha-linolenic acid, a plant omega-3 fat, though not in amounts that make mallow a rich source of it.
  • In traditional herbal practice, mallow is also used as a mild, soothing diuretic — a substance that gently increases urine flow.
  • On the skin, mallow has a long traditional use as a softening poultice or wash for irritated, inflamed skin, minor wounds and insect bites; early laboratory and animal research supports its soothing, wound-supporting character, but it has not been well tested in people.

Soil & Environment

  • Its strong roots help hold soil in place on banks and disturbed ground.
  • The flowers draw bees and other pollinating insects through a long flowering season.
  • Mallow is unusually sensitive to ozone, a form of air pollution: its leaves show visible speckled injury at levels other plants tolerate, so scientists have used it as a living warning sign — a bioindicator — of ozone in the air.

Mullein

Verbascum thapsus

A classic pioneer of open, disturbed ground — dry, sandy or gravelly soils, rail banks and waste places. Its tall yellow flower spikes are visited by bees through the summer.

Health Benefits

  • The flowers are a traditional remedy for the symptoms of sore throat that come with a dry cough and a cold — this is the use in the European Medicines Agency's herbal monograph, and it rests on long-standing use, not clinical trials.
  • The soothing effect is the flower's mucilage — a few percent of slippery, coating plant fibres that calm an irritated throat.
  • Mullein leaf and flower have a long tradition in teas for coughs and chest complaints generally.
  • Herbal ear drops containing mullein eased children's ear pain as well as anaesthetic drops in two clinical trials — but mullein was one of several herbs in olive oil, so the effect cannot be credited to mullein alone.
  • Strain mullein teas well, through fine cloth: the fine hairs on the leaves can irritate the throat and mouth.
  • Caution: the seeds are poisonous and must never be eaten — they were once used to stun fish. The EMA does not recommend mullein flower for children under 12, or in pregnancy and while breastfeeding, where its safety has not been established, and advises using it for no more than a week at a time.

Soil & Environment

  • A classic pioneer of open, disturbed ground — dry, sandy or gravelly soils, rail banks and waste places — covering bare soil where little else will grow.
  • Its seed is famously long-lived: in a buried-seed experiment begun in 1879, mullein seeds were still sprouting after more than 140 years, and a single plant can leave a seed bank that outlasts the gardener.
  • The tall yellow flower spikes are visited by bees and other pollinators through the summer, and the seed is eaten by finches and sparrows, including the American goldfinch.
  • Left standing, the dead stems shelter overwintering insects, and the plant hosts the mullein moth. It spreads freely and is classed as a noxious weed in Colorado and Hawaii, so it needs watching where it is not wanted.

Nettle

Urtica dioica

The stinging nettle is a tall perennial of rich, damp ground, native to temperate Europe and Asia and now found worldwide; its hollow hairs act like tiny needles, injecting a sting that cooking or drying completely removes. It is a whole wardrobe of a plant — leaf, root, stem fibre and seed are all used — and one of the most nutritious wild greens there is. Its genus holds roughly 80 species, and the wider nettle family over 2,600.

Health Benefits

  • The leaf is a genuinely rich food: high in vitamins A, C and K, calcium, iron and protein for a leafy green, and full of antioxidant plant compounds.
  • The root — not the leaf — is the part used for the prostate: nettle root preparations are traditionally used, and recognised by European regulators, for easing the urinary symptoms of a benign enlarged prostate (such as a weak stream and frequent night-time trips), once a doctor has ruled out anything serious. A large six-month trial found root extract improved those symptoms more than a placebo, though the evidence overall is still rated limited.
  • The leaf is traditionally used as a mild diuretic, increasing urine flow to flush the urinary tract in minor urinary complaints — a use European regulators accept on the basis of long-standing practice.
  • The leaf is also a traditional remedy for minor joint pain, and rubbing the fresh plant on painful joints is an old folk practice that one small trial found helpful for thumb-joint arthritis pain.
  • For hay fever, nettle leaf is often called a natural antihistamine: in laboratory tests its extract blocks histamine receptors, and one small early trial of freeze-dried leaf suggested it may ease symptoms — promising, but not yet well proven.
  • The leaf has a traditional use as a rinse for an oily scalp and hair.

Soil & Environment

  • Nettle patches are nurseries for butterflies: the caterpillars of the small tortoiseshell, peacock, red admiral and comma butterflies feed on nettle leaves, and few other plants can replace it for them.
  • A patch also shelters aphids early in spring, which gives ladybirds and other aphid-eating insects an early meal and gets them established before garden pests build up.
  • The stem fibres are strong enough to spin and weave — nettle cloth is thousands of years old, and the fibre has been used for textiles, rope and fishing nets, and as animal bedding and feed.
  • Chopped nettles are rich in nitrogen and minerals, which makes them a fine compost activator and the base of the traditional nettle liquid feed for the garden.
  • Dried nettle is a nutritious animal feed, fed to poultry and livestock.

Plantain

Plantago major

Broadleaf plantain — no relation to the banana-like fruit — is a low rosette of wide, ribbed leaves native to Europe and Asia that has followed people around the world, springing up in lawns, paths and compacted ground everywhere. The leaf is the main medicinal part; the seeds are used too.

Health Benefits

  • The leaf is the classic wayside first-aid plant: bruised fresh and laid on the skin, or made into a salve, it has been used for centuries on minor wounds, scrapes and insect bites. It contains allantoin, a compound that supports skin repair, and aucubin, which acts against microbes; a small clinical study of a plantain ointment on burns found it performed comparably to a standard treatment cream.
  • The leaf’s tannins — the astringent compounds that make strong tea taste dry — and its flavonoid antioxidants are behind its traditional use for digestive upsets. In animal studies, plantain extracts have protected the stomach lining, but plantain has not been shown to prevent stomach ulcers in people.
  • In laboratory tests, plantain extracts act against a range of bacteria and some viruses. That is a laboratory finding, not proof that the plant fights infections in the body.
  • The leaf is traditionally used in skin washes and toners, where its astringent tannins gently tighten and cleanse the skin’s surface.
  • The seeds are rich in mucilage, a swelling fibre similar to psyllium (which comes from a close plantain relative), traditionally used to keep the bowels moving gently.

Soil & Environment

  • Its strong fibrous roots and ground-hugging rosette help bind soil and slow erosion, and the plant tolerates the compacted, trampled ground where little else will grow.
  • The dense mat of leaves shades the soil surface, helping it hold moisture.
  • In laboratory studies, an extract of plantain seed worked as a natural coagulant — a substance that makes fine particles in dirty water clump together so they can be removed — clearing industrial wastewater about as well as some conventional treatments.

Purple Dead-Nettle

Lamium purpureum

A member of the mint family. The “dead” in its name means stingless — unlike its cousin the stinging nettle, it has no stinging hairs and is safe to handle. It is one of the earliest flowers of the year.

Health Benefits

  • A member of the mint family. The “dead” in its name means stingless — unlike its cousin the stinging nettle, it has no stinging hairs and is safe to handle.
  • The whole plant above ground is edible — leaves, stems and flowers — with a mild, earthy taste, and the leaves provide vitamins A, C and K, iron and fibre.
  • In European folk tradition it was used mostly on the skin, as a poultice or wash for cuts, scrapes and minor irritation.
  • Folk practice also describes it as astringent and as a gentle diuretic, though these are traditional descriptions, not tested ones.
  • It is a gentle folk herb rather than a tested medicine — research on this species itself is still very limited, and most laboratory work has been done on its relatives.
  • Caution: large amounts — a strong tea, for example — can have a laxative effect. Take care with identification: its closest lookalikes are henbit, which is also edible, and ground ivy, and while none of the usual lookalikes is toxic, it is still worth waiting for the square stem, the purple-tinged top leaves and the pink-purple flowers to be sure you have the right plant.

Soil & Environment

  • One of the earliest flowers of the year, blooming from late winter — a critical nectar source for queen bumblebees emerging from hibernation, and for honeybees and digger bees, when almost nothing else is in flower.
  • Its crimson-red pollen is an early source of protein for bees raising their first brood.
  • A winter annual, it covers bare soil through the cold months and then recedes by summer, making room for later plants.
  • Its seeds carry a small oily parcel (an elaiosome, a fatty attachment that ants eat) that ants carry off, planting the seed as they go.

Purslane

Portulaca oleracea

Purslane is a fleshy, low-spreading succulent with spoon-shaped leaves and small yellow flowers, eaten as a vegetable since ancient times and now found in gardens and warm ground all over the world; its exact wild origin is debated, somewhere in the Old World. Leaves, stems and seeds are all edible — and it is one of the most nutrient-packed plants in the garden.

Health Benefits

  • Purslane leaves carry more alpha-linolenic acid — the plant form of omega-3 fat — than any other leafy green vegetable measured, about 300–400 mg in a 100 g serving. (The body converts only a little of this plant omega-3 into the fish-oil forms, EPA and DHA.)
  • It is mineral-rich for a leafy green: a good source of potassium and magnesium, with useful amounts of calcium, plus vitamins A, C and E.
  • In animal studies, purslane extracts have protected the liver against damage. This effect has not been demonstrated in people.
  • In animal studies, purslane extracts have also protected brain and nerve cells — the kind of early research sometimes described as neuroprotective. This, too, has not been shown in people.

Soil & Environment

  • It is a pioneer plant, among the first to take hold on bare, poor or dry ground, covering and stabilising soil where little else will grow.
  • Its dense, fleshy mat shades the soil and slows evaporation, helping the ground beneath it hold water.
  • Gardeners often let it grow as a living ground cover among taller crops — it is a recognised companion beneath corn, tomatoes, peppers and potatoes, keeping the soil cool and moist around them while its deeper roots draw up minerals from below.
  • Its small flowers are visited by bees and other small pollinating insects.

Red Clover

Trifolium pratense

A nitrogen-fixing legume: bacteria in its root nodules take nitrogen from the air and add it to the soil, which is why farmers grow it as a cover crop and green manure.

Health Benefits

  • Contains isoflavones — plant compounds, including biochanin A and formononetin, that are structurally similar to the hormone estrogen.
  • Traditionally used for skin conditions such as eczema and psoriasis, and for coughs, including whooping cough.
  • Supplements are promoted for menopausal hot flushes, but studies have had inconsistent results, and the US National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health finds no conclusive evidence of benefit for any condition.
  • Caution: because of its estrogen-like activity, avoid red clover supplements in pregnancy and while breastfeeding, and talk to a healthcare provider first if you have a hormone-sensitive condition or take hormone-related medication.
  • Caution: red clover may increase the risk of bleeding alongside blood-thinning medicines such as warfarin. The safety of long-term use is not known.

Soil & Environment

  • A nitrogen-fixing legume: bacteria in its root nodules take nitrogen from the air and add it to the soil, which is why farmers grow it as a cover crop and green manure.
  • Its flowers are pollinated by bumblebees and honeybees, and it is an important nectar plant and source of honey.
  • A deep taproot helps break up compacted layers of soil.
  • Grown as a living cover, it protects the soil surface from erosion.

Roselle

Hibiscus sabdariffa

This is Jamaican sorrel — the plant behind the ruby-red Christmas drink. It is a hibiscus, and no relation at all to wood sorrel or to garden sorrel: three different plants that share a name because they share a tart taste. The part used is the fleshy red calyx — the cup that swells around the seed pod after the flower falls.

Health Benefits

  • This is Jamaican sorrel — the plant behind the ruby-red Christmas drink. It is a hibiscus, and no relation at all to wood sorrel or to garden sorrel: three different plants that share a name because they share a tart taste.
  • The part used is the fleshy red calyx — the cup that swells around the seed pod after the flower falls. Dried and brewed, it makes the cooling drinks known across the tropics as sorrel in the Caribbean, bissap in Senegal, karkadé in Egypt and Sudan, zobo in Nigeria and agua de Jamaica in Mexico. The calyces are rich in vitamin C and in the red anthocyanin pigments that give the drink its colour.
  • Blood pressure is its most studied use. Pooled analyses of the trials find that hibiscus tea and extracts lower blood pressure modestly — on average around seven points off the top reading — with the clearest effects in people whose pressure was raised to begin with. The US National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health stresses that the evidence is limited and the effects small beside prescribed medicines. Roselle is not a treatment for high blood pressure, and it must never be used in place of prescribed medication.
  • It has also been studied for cholesterol and blood sugar, with weaker and mixed results: a pooled review of the cholesterol trials found no significant effect, and the blood-sugar findings are inconsistent. Neither is a claim this page can make.
  • Caution: avoid roselle in pregnancy. It has never been properly tested in pregnant women, and animal studies raise concerns that strong extracts can affect the womb and the development of offspring. Its safety while breastfeeding is likewise unknown.
  • Caution: roselle can add to the effect of blood-pressure medicines, so pressure may fall too far — talk to a clinician first if you take them. It can sharply reduce how much of the antimalarial medicine chloroquine the body absorbs, and it can change how the body handles some other medicines, including the cholesterol medicine simvastatin. Take care alongside diabetes medicines, as it may lower blood sugar a little, and note that very large doses of strong extracts have raised liver enzymes in animal studies.

Soil & Environment

  • A plant of West Africa originally, carried across the tropics centuries ago and now grown wherever summers are long and hot — Sudan, Nigeria, Mexico, Egypt, Thailand and China are among the biggest producers.
  • A true tropical annual: sown after all frost has passed, it needs full sun, warmth and a long season, grows fast to two metres or more in good, well-drained soil, and is killed by the first frost.
  • The harvest is gathered by hand — the fleshy red calyces are picked as they swell around the seed pods. The stems have a second use, yielding a jute-like fibre.
  • The large flowers each open for a single day and are visited by bees and other pollinating insects through the flowering season.

Self-Heal

Prunella vulgaris

One of the great traditional wound herbs — its old name is “heal-all” — used for centuries across Europe, Asia and North America for wounds, sores and sore throats. In Chinese medicine the dried flower spike, called xiakucao, is a long-standing traditional remedy in its own right.

Health Benefits

  • One of the great traditional wound herbs — its old name is “heal-all” — used for centuries across Europe, Asia and North America for wounds, sores and sore throats.
  • In Chinese medicine the dried flower spike, called xiakucao, is a long-standing traditional remedy in its own right.
  • The plant contains rosmarinic acid, ursolic acid and flavonoids, and in laboratory studies these show anti-inflammatory, antioxidant and antimicrobial activity — interesting chemistry, but laboratory results are not the same as benefit in people.
  • There are no solid human trials behind self-heal, so every use on this page stays a traditional one.
  • It has a gentle safety record in traditional use; a mild stomach upset is possible.
  • Caution: its safety in pregnancy and while breastfeeding has not been studied, so medicinal use is best avoided then.

Soil & Environment

  • Its fibrous, creeping roots and spreading habit hold soil together — the US Department of Agriculture recommends it for erosion control along roadsides, stream banks and pond edges.
  • The purple flower spikes give nectar and pollen to butterflies — whites, sulphurs and skippers — and to native bees, including bumblebees, digger bees, small carpenter bees and sweat bees.
  • Ground-foraging birds eat the small nut-like seeds, and the caterpillars of the gray marvel moth feed on the leaves.
  • A low, lawn-friendly plant that stays green, flowers even when mown short, and spreads into a living ground cover — though it can become weedy if left entirely unmanaged.

Shepherd's Purse

Capsella bursa-pastoris

One of the commonest weeds in the world — a pioneer of disturbed ground, gardens, paths and field edges, and one of the first plants to colonise bare soil. It can flower in almost any month of the year.

Health Benefits

  • European regulators recognise shepherd's purse as a traditional herbal medicine for reducing heavy menstrual bleeding in women whose cycles are regular — and only after a doctor has ruled out serious causes. Heavy bleeding always needs checking first.
  • Following the monograph, it is taken starting three to five days before the period is due.
  • In two small clinical trials, an extract added to a standard painkiller (mefenamic acid) reduced menstrual bleeding more than the painkiller alone, with the clearest difference by the third month — promising, but early results from small studies.
  • Its fame as a herb that stops bleeding is centuries old — it was used on wounds in the First World War — but European assessors found no studies showing an actual clotting effect, so that reputation is history, not established fact.
  • Caution: the EMA does not recommend shepherd's purse in pregnancy or while breastfeeding — extracts made womb tissue contract in laboratory studies, and its safety in pregnancy has never been tested. It is also not recommended for anyone under 18.
  • Caution: because this is traditionally a bleeding herb, take care alongside blood-thinning medicines and check with a clinician first — though it is fair to say the EMA itself reports no documented interactions and no known side effects.

Soil & Environment

  • One of the commonest weeds in the world — a pioneer of disturbed ground, gardens, paths and field edges, and one of the first plants to colonise bare soil.
  • It can flower in almost any month of the year, and its early spring flowers feed small bees and flies when little else is out.
  • Birds are fond of the seeds — chaffinches and other small birds eat them — and the seed can survive in the soil for decades, waiting for its chance.
  • It is being studied for cleaning contaminated soils, as part of phytoremediation research — using plants to draw contaminants out of soil.

Sorrel (Wood Sorrel)

Oxalis spp.

This entry is about wood sorrel — the little lawn weed with three heart-shaped leaflets — not the garden salad herb of the same name. Jamaican sorrel is a different plant again — a hibiscus, sharing nothing with this one but the name and the sharp taste.

Health Benefits

  • This page is about wood sorrel — the little lawn weed with three heart-shaped leaflets — not the garden salad herb of the same name. Jamaican sorrel is a different plant again — a hibiscus, sharing nothing with this one but the name and the sharp taste.
  • The leaves, flowers and young seed pods are eaten in small amounts as a wayside nibble; the sharp, lemony taste comes from oxalic acid.
  • It is a traditional thirst-quencher and spring green, chewed on walks to freshen the mouth.
  • It contains vitamin C, and greens like it were once valued in spring against scurvy — that is history and tradition, not a modern treatment claim.
  • Caution: the same oxalic acid that gives the flavour binds calcium and can interfere with absorbing it, so wood sorrel is a small-amounts plant only. People with kidney stones, gout, kidney problems or rheumatoid arthritis should avoid it or be especially careful. Cooking reduces the soluble oxalates.
  • Caution for pets and livestock: all wood sorrels are listed as toxic to dogs, cats and horses. A nibble usually causes no more than drooling and an upset stomach, but grazing animals that eat a lot of it are at real risk.

Soil & Environment

  • The ripe seed capsules are spring-loaded — touched, they fling their seeds several feet, which is one reason wood sorrel turns up everywhere.
  • It spreads by seed and by thin underground stems, and will grow in poor, disturbed and compacted soils, in anything from full sun to shade.
  • The small yellow flowers are visited by small bees through a long season.
  • It makes a quick living cover on bare, poor ground where little else volunteers — though that same enthusiasm means it needs watching in beds where it is not wanted.

St. John's Wort

Hypericum perforatum

A sunny meadow and wayside perennial whose flowers offer no nectar but provide abundant pollen, gathered by bees through the summer. Its medicine interactions are the first thing to know about it.

Health Benefits

  • Caution — prescription medicines: St. John's wort speeds up the liver enzymes that clear many medicines from the body, so those medicines can stop working. The list includes the contraceptive pill — breakthrough bleeding and unplanned pregnancies have been reported — transplant anti-rejection medicines, HIV medicines, cancer medicines, the blood thinner warfarin, digoxin, and some antidepressants, whose levels it lowers.
  • Caution — serotonin syndrome: combined with antidepressant medicines (SSRIs, SNRIs and triptans among them), St. John's wort can cause serotonin syndrome — a potentially life-threatening reaction with agitation, fever, fast heartbeat, confusion and muscle stiffness. Anyone taking any prescription medicine must talk to a doctor or pharmacist before using it.
  • St. John's wort has been studied for mild to moderate depression, with mixed results: a large Cochrane review of 29 trials found its extracts worked better than placebo and about as well as standard antidepressants for mild to moderate depression, with fewer side effects — but two large US trials in major depression found no benefit over placebo, and the US National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health calls the evidence not definitive. Depression is a serious illness that needs medical care; this is not a casual tonic, and it is never a substitute for treatment.
  • European regulators accept specified extracts for mild to moderate depressive episodes as a well-established use, and list traditional uses for temporary mental exhaustion, minor skin inflammation such as sunburn and minor wounds (as an infused oil), and mild digestive discomfort.
  • Caution: it increases the skin's sensitivity to sunlight, and that applies to the infused oil on the skin as well as to taking it. It may be unsafe in pregnancy, and European regulators do not recommend it for anyone under 18.

Soil & Environment

  • A sunny meadow and wayside perennial whose flowers offer no nectar but provide abundant pollen, gathered by bees through the summer.
  • An honest downside: away from its home ground it can take over — it is classed as a noxious weed in several western US states and parts of Australia, spreads by seed and creeping roots, and is poisonous to grazing livestock, making their skin painfully sensitive to sunlight.
  • In California it was famously brought back under control by two leaf beetles introduced from Europe — one of the classic success stories of biological weed control.
  • Its deep roots can hold soil on banks, but its spreading habit means it needs watching wherever it is planted.

Violet

Viola spp.

The leaves and flowers are the parts used — in syrups, teas and salads, and in skin preparations. Among the earliest spring flowers, violets feed emerging bees, including a mining bee that specialises in violets.

Health Benefits

  • The leaves and flowers are the parts used — in syrups, teas and salads, and in skin preparations. (The roots and seeds are a different matter — see the caution below.)
  • Violet syrup is a traditional cough remedy, long given for coughs and sore throats, especially to children.
  • In one small double-blind trial, violet syrup eased the cough of children aged 2–12 with intermittent asthma when added to their usual treatment — a single small study, offered as encouragement, not proof.
  • The leaves provide vitamins A and C, and the plant's slippery mucilage is soothing to irritated throats and skin — the traditional reason it is classed as a demulcent, a substance that coats and soothes.
  • Violet contains natural salicylate compounds, the aspirin family of chemicals, which fits its old folk use for aches and inflamed skin.
  • Caution: only the leaves and flowers are eaten. The roots and seeds are not food — the root contains an alkaloid, violine, and is strongly sickening and purgative in larger doses, and some violets are high in saponins, which can cause nausea if too much is eaten.

Soil & Environment

  • Violets are the only food of fritillary caterpillars — the great spangled, variegated and regal fritillaries all feed exclusively on violet leaves, so a patch of violets is a butterfly nursery.
  • The seeds carry a small oily parcel (an elaiosome, a fatty attachment that ants eat) that ants carry back to their nests, planting the violets in rich nest soil as they go.
  • Among the earliest spring flowers, they feed emerging bees — including a mining bee, Andrena violae, that specialises in violets.
  • A shade-tolerant ground cover that spreads by runners and self-seeding, holding soil under trees and along shady edges where grass struggles.

Yarrow

Achillea millefolium

One of the oldest traditional wound herbs — long used on small cuts and superficial wounds, and known as “soldier's woundwort” from its battlefield history.

Health Benefits

  • One of the oldest traditional wound herbs — long used on small cuts and superficial wounds, and known as “soldier's woundwort” from its battlefield history. European regulators list this among its traditional uses.
  • Traditionally used for temporary loss of appetite, and for mild, cramping digestive complaints such as bloating and wind.
  • Traditionally used for minor cramps associated with menstrual periods.
  • These uses are traditional, based on long-standing use: the European Medicines Agency notes there is not enough clinical-trial evidence to confirm them.
  • Caution: do not use if you are allergic to plants in the daisy family, such as ragweed or chamomile. Avoid in pregnancy and for children under 12, and take care alongside blood-thinning medicines, as yarrow can add to their effect — check with a clinician first.

Soil & Environment

  • The flat flower heads attract pollinators and beneficial insects, including hoverflies, ladybirds, lacewings and tachinid flies that help control garden pests.
  • Its flowers also feed butterflies and other wildlife.
  • Used in land restoration, and for erosion control on steep slopes.
  • Drought tolerant once established, and grows in dry, poor soils where many plants struggle.
